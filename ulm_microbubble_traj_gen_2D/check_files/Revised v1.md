   The vessel radius determines how close a microbubble can get to the wall; the flow rate dictates its traveling speed; viscosity and flow velocity determine the strength of the drag force exerted by the blood flow; and the wall shear stress determines whether the microbubble will be washed away even after adhering to the wall.

   The optimization of the vascular network generation and the subsequent simulation of microbubble behavior are completely decoupled and independent of each other\.

$\tau_w = \frac{4\mu Q}{\pi R^3}$

The above equation is specifically used to calculate the wall shear stress of a steady laminar flow \(Hagen\-Poiseuille flow\) of an incompressible fluid in a circular pipe\.

When a fluid flows over a solid surface, the layer of fluid molecules immediately adjacent to the wall is adsorbed by it, and its velocity relative to the wall drops to zero\. However, the fluid slightly further away from the wall continues to flow rapidly\. The internal friction generated by the fluid's viscosity is ultimately transmitted through this wall\-adjacent fluid layer and applied to the solid surface as a tangential pulling force, which is known as wall shear stress\. The fluid must overcome this wall shear stress to flow forward\. This friction results in a pressure drop\.

2. Upon entering a blood vessel, a microbubble should first be treated as a particle transported by the blood flow\. Each microbubble should possess its own radius, injection time, initial vessel segment, initial cross\-sectional position, functionalization status, surface ligand density, and non\-specific adhesion tendency\. Real microbubbles cannot be perfectly identical; therefore, these attributes should follow a statistical distribution rather than all microbubbles sharing a single fixed value\.

   In a straight vessel segment, microbubbles primarily move along the axial direction of the vessel\. This can be described using a Poiseuille velocity profile: microbubbles closer to the vessel center travel faster, while those closer to the vessel wall travel slower\.

   $u_i(r) = 2\bar{U}_i \left(1 - \frac{r^2}{R_i^2}\right)$

   $\bar{U}_i = \frac{Q_i}{\pi R_i^2}$

   Where $r$ is the radius distance of microbubble from the cross\-section center\. In vascular tunnels, without additional lateral disturbance, the microbubble should not move near to the wall actively. Only if microbubble is near the wall, can it attached to that.
3. There are two reasons for microbubble approaching the vessel wall, which are initial cross\-section distribution and bifurcation geometric disturbance\.

   Initial distribution means that when microbubbles first enter a vessel or chip inlet, they are not necessarily all in the exact center of the channel\. They may be evenly distributed, off\-center, or near the wall\. This initial position strongly influences whether they will subsequently contact the wall\. Because in laminar flow, microbubbles typically move along their own streamline; microbubbles that start near the wall streamline are more likely to become candidates for subsequent binding events\.

   Bifurcation geometric perturbation is the second source\. The bifurcation structures are not only part of the vascular morphology, but they also affect the positional changes of microvesicles as they pass through the bifurcation\. When microvesicles enter children vessels from parent vessels, the flow lines are redistributed, and some microvesicles may be carried to the vicinity of the medial or lateral wall of the daughter vessel, thus creating opportunities for proximal wall contact\.

   We should not simply set an empirical probability of 10% of microbubbles near the wall\. A better approach is to create **a transfer map** \. This describes which sub\-vessel a microbubble will enter after starting from a certain cross\-section of the parent vessel, passing through a certain bifurcation, and where it will appear within the cross\-section of the sub\-vessel\.

   - Use **FEniCS** or other local flow field simulations to calculate the streamline transfer in typical bifurcation structures\.

   In this way, the near\-wall entry is generated by the inlet distribution, bifurcation angle, radius ratio, and flow distribution\.
