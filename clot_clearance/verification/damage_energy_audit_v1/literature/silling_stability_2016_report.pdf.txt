Abstract
Peridynamic correspondence material models provide a way to combine a material model
from the local theory with the inherent capabilities of peridynamics to model long-range forces
and fracture. However, correspondence models in a typical particle discretization suffer from
zero-energy mode instability. These instabilities are shown here to be an aspect of material
stability. A stability condition is derived for state-based materials starting from the requirement
of potential energy minimization. It is shown that all correspondence materials fail this stability
condition due to zero-energy deformation modes of the family. To eliminate these modes, a term
is added to the correspondence strain energy density that resists deviations from a uniform
deformation. The resulting material model satisfies the stability condition while effectively
leaving the stress tensor unchanged. Computational examples demonstrate the effectiveness of
the modified material model in avoiding zero-energy mode instability in a peridynamic particle
code.

1 Introduction
Peridynamics is a nonlocal theory of mechanics in which material points in a continuum or in a
group of discrete particles interact with each other through forces. The exchange of forces between
any pair of material points is called a bond interaction. These forces are determined by the material
model according to the current deformation.
Material models in peridynamics explicitly determine the force in each bond. Many material
models have been investigated as the peridynamic theory has been developed. A particularly
useful class of material models is called the correspondence models. These models use a stress-
strain relation from the local theory as a means to determine the bond forces. In principle, any
material model from a finite element code can be applied directly in a peridynamic computational
simulation when formulated as a correspondence model.
Correspondence material models have been used successfully in the modeling of a wide variety of
material response, including elastic-plastic and viscoplastic materials with large strains and fracture,
for example [6, 5, 21]. However, the usefulness of peridynamic correspondence models has been
impeded by some practical issues in their implementation in meshless peridynamic codes. One such
issue is the difficulty that is sometimes encountered when evaluating the shape tensor defined in
equation (15) below, particularly when very large deformations or extensive damage are present. A
more troublesome issue with correspondence models is their tendency to exhibit zero-energy mode
oscillations in meshless simulations. These oscillations typically build up gradually during a long
simulation, but they can eventually ruin the results. They can occur even when the appropriate
version [16] of the Courant-Friedrichs-Levy time step restriction is applied. Zero-energy mode
instabilities can occur in a variety of particle methods as well as finite elements, motivating the
development of corrective techniques [4, 20, 9]. The purpose of the present paper is to investigate
the root cause of this type of instability as a manifestation of a material instability, rather than
merely an artifact of the meshless discretization. A stabilized correspondence material model is
proposed that is stable in both the continuum and discretized equations.

2 Peridynamics background
Peridynamics is a generalization of the standard theory of solid mechanics. It treats the mechanics
of continuous bodies and discrete particles, including long-range forces and fracture, within the
same basic field equations. A typical material point x in a body 8 interacts with other material

is called the horizon, S. The vector in the undeformed configuration from x to any of its neighbors
q E 7-t is called a bond, generically given the symbol = q — x. The vector = 0 is excluded from
the family.

The vector-valued function f(q, x, t) is the pairwise bond force density (with units of force per
volume squared) that q exerts on x. The material model determines the values of f according to
the deformations of the families of x and of q.
The simplest class of material model [14] is called bond-based, in which f(q, x, t) depends only
on the deformation of the bond q — x. More general material response can be incorporated using
the state-based class of models [17], in which f(q, x, t) depends not only on q — x, but also on
the deformation of all the other bonds in H. State-based material models are expressed using
mathematical objects called states, which are mappings from H to some other quantity, either
vector- or scalar-valued. By convention, the bond that a state A operates on is written in angle
brackets, A(). Dependence of the state on position and time is written in square brackets, A[x, t].
The deformation state Y maps bonds onto their deformed images:

As mentioned in the Introduction, computational simulations using correspondence material
models are susceptible to weak instabilities due to zero-energy ocsillatory modes. These have been
reported in several publications and are commonly seen in practice. Ganzenmfiller, Hiermaier, and
May [7] interpret the zero-energy modes in meshless simulations as an aspect of the numerical
discretization, drawing on similarities with rank deficiency effects in Smoothed-Particle Hydrody-
namics (SPH). Closely related results are discussed by Bessa, Foster, Belytschko, and Liu [1].
Numerical stabilization techniques are commonly used with correspondence material models
to suppress the zero-energy modes. Littlewood [10, 11] introduced corrective forces on the bonds
that tend to drive nodes in a meshless model toward smooth deformations. Breitenfeld, Geubelle,
Weckner, and Silling [2] proposed stabilizing forces that push a material particle closer to the
geometrical center of its deformed family. Damping forces and artificial viscosity are also somewhat
effective in reducing zero-energy modes [13]. Tupek and Radovitzky [19] investigated the use of
alternative strain measures to help avoid the closely related phenomenon of interpenetration in
meshless peridynamic grids.
Up to now, the phenomenon of zero-energy modes in correspondence materials has generally
been viewed as a consequence of meshless discretization. Instead, the purpose of the present work
is to study the root cause of this phenomenon as a material instability rather than a numerical
instability [8]. To pursue this, a general criterion for material stability in peridynamics will first be
discussed.

7 Examples
Three examples are presented here. All three model an aluminum coupon with dimensions 150mm
x 50mm x 5mm that contains a hole with diameter 20mm. It is modeled using a peridynamic
correspondence material model. The grid spacing is lmm. Other than the modification to the
material model described in the previous sections, these calculations did not use damping or other
stabilization forces. The particle discretization and explicit time integration method are described
in [16].
In the first example, the plate is modeled as linear elastic. Figure 1 compares the velocity
contours on the midplane of the coupon after being stretched at constant rate to a nominal tensile
strain of 0.02%. The velocity fields with and without stabilization of the correspondence material
model are shown. The zero-energy mode oscillations are evident in the left plot, without the
stabilization. The stabilization term is obtained from (55) with a = 1. The zero-energy mode
oscillations are apparently eliminated when the stabilized material model is used, as shown in the
right plot.
In the second example, the plate is modeled as a brittle linear elastic material with a bond
failure strain of 0.2%. Bond breakage is implemented by setting the influence function c.0() to zero
when the bond breaks. Cracks nucleate at the stress concentrations near the hole and immediately
grow toward the free edges of the specimen. The calculation uses the stabilized correspondence
material model with a = 1. Figure 2 shows the deformed grid and contours of damage after failure
of the coupon. There is no evidence of instability in the result, and apparently the stabilization
forces do not interfere with the progression of damage.
In the third example, the same coupon is modeled as elastic-plastic using the peridynamic
implementation of plasticity described in [21]. The flow stress includes the effect of strain hardening
using the Johnson-Cook plasticity model. Stabilization is used as described above. Necking occurs
near the hole in the coupon. This simulation demonstrates that physically reasonable results
without zero-energy mode instability are obtained using the stabilization method, even with fairly
large plastic strain. The results are shown in Figure 3.

8 Conclusions
It was shown in this paper that any correspondence material model i c is always unstable in
the sense of (31). The instability manifests itself as zero-energy mode oscillations in a particle
discretization.
However, the modified material model in T given by (47) is stable. From the discussion in
Section 3, this result applies equally to continuum and discrete models. The stabilized correspon-
dence material model satisfies the requirements of peridynamic material models (objectivity and
non-polarity) and is elastic. The stabilization forces do not change the partial stress tensor. The
new terms require negligible computer resources. The numerical examples suggest that this ap-
proach to stabilization is compatible with elastic and inelastic correspondence models and with
bond breakage as a means to incorporate damage.
