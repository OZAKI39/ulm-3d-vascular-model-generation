ABSTRACT
Despite the high mortality rates associated with thromboembolic diseases, computational modeling of the physics of thrombo -
embolism remains underdeveloped in the literature due to the inadequacy of classical finite element methods to accommodate 
the growth, large deformation, and fracture of blood clots, especially under the influence of fluid dynamic forces. Accordingly, 
we present a meshless numerical framework, employing peridynamics (PD) that readily captures the constitutive response, 
damage progression, and eventual failure of a blood clot. The PD framework was validated against three benchmark test cases: 
tensile loading of a plate with a hole, torsional loading of a column, and tensile loading of thin structural plates both with and 
without notches. Comparative quantitative and qualitative analysis demonstrated excellent agreement with finite element solu -
tions generated using the commercial software ANSYS. The validated framework was then used to calibrate the peridynamic 
parameters to accurately reproduce the mechanical response, the cohesive bulk fracture of blood clots under tensile loading, and 
the debonding of blood clots from artificial surfaces, including titanium (Ti), polyurethane (PU), and polytetrafluoroethylene 
(PTFE). Force–displacement curves obtained using these calibrated parameters demonstrated a strong correlation with experi -
mental data.
1   |   Introduction
Thromboembolic diseases, characterized by the formation and 
fragmentation of blood clots under hemodynamic forces, are es -
timated to contribute to one in four deaths globally [ 1]. These 
conditions are lethal due to the potential of clot fragments to 
obstruct blood flow to vital organs, leading to life-  threatening 
complications, including ischemic stroke and pulmonary embo -
lism [2]. A mechanistic understanding of thromboembolism is 
crucial for gaining insights into its underlying physics, which 
remains largely unexplored and poorly understood [ 3]. The 
aforementioned research gap stems from the computational 
complexity inherent to thromboembolism modeling, which ne -
cessitates an accurate representation of fracture processes in 
highly deformable, dynamically growing blood clots subjected 
to fluid shear. The present work provides a critical foundation 
for a comprehensive numerical framework for thromboembo -
lism by addressing the soft material fracture mechanics aspect 
of this coupled phenomenon.
The most prevalent numerical framework used to simulate soft 
material fracture is the cohesive zone model (CZM), typically 
implemented using the finite element (FE) method [ 4, 5]. CZM 
has been very successful in capturing complex crack patterns 
in soft materials such as elastomers [ 6]. However, explicit track-
ing of stresses around the crack tip is required for crack branch 
determination, which can incur significant computational costs 
when applied to the modeling of complex failure mechanisms

of growing blood clots. In recent years, phase field methods 
(PFMs) have gained popularity for simulating fracture in diverse 
materials [7], including biological materials such as arteries [ 8] 
and blood clots [ 9]. This approach models fracture through the 
minimization of an energy functional that combines elastic and 
fracture energy contributions [ 10]. In essence, it conceptualizes 
failure as a damage accumulation process and, when damage 
exceeds a certain threshold, a crack surface is formed. Although 
this circumvents the drawback of traditional FE methods by 
removing the need to track cracked surfaces, it requires highly 
refined meshes in regions near a crack. Given the inherent het -
erogeneity of blood clots and the uncertain fluid loading patterns 
encountered in vivo, a priori determination of crack propagation 
paths is difficult, thereby requiring extremely fine mesh discret -
izations when using phase-  field models for thromboembolism 
simulation. Comparative analysis by Song et  al. demonstrated 
that traditional FE methods exhibit mesh dependence arising 
from local fracture criterion implementation [ 11]. This limita -
tion has been successfully addressed through non-  local fracture 
criteria employing regularization length scales to prevent dam -
age localization within thin element bands, as demonstrated 
in both FE analysis [ 12] and phase-  field modeling [ 8]. Given 
these advances, one might reasonably expect that state-  of- the- 
art mesh- based fracture models incorporating non-  local criteria 
would be sufficient for thromboembolism modeling. However, 
a more fundamental computational challenge arises for throm -
boembolism, as all these methods require a mesh. Mesh-  based 
discretization becomes computationally intractable when mod -
eling dynamically evolving blood clots due to the dual require -
ments of real-  time element connectivity generation and highly 
refined mesh resolution for accurate crack propagation. This 
computational burden renders mesh-  based approaches imprac -
tical for simulating thromboembolism. Therefore, meshless 
methodologies are necessary to effectively model the deforma -
tion and fracture processes of clots.
In recent years, peridynamics (PD), a non-  local formulation of 
classical continuum mechanics (CCM) introduced by Silling, 
has gained significant traction in the field of computational frac-
ture mechanics [13]. This innovative approach models material 
behavior by considering interactions between material points 
through a finite set of bonds in an interaction domain. Damage 
in PD is modeled through breaking bonds between material 
points based on kinetic or kinematic criteria, allowing fracture 
to emerge as a natural consequence of the dynamics of the sys -
tem. Furthermore, the governing equation of PD is formulated 
as an integral equation, which confers a significant advantage 
for handling material discontinuities. This mathematical struc -
ture inherently avoids the singularities that arise in CCM when 
dealing with discontinuities such as near cracks or interfaces. 
Given these significant advantages, this study adopts PD as the 
preferred numerical framework for simulating blood clot frac -
ture. In particular, the Non-  ordinary State-  based formulation of 
PD is adopted. The justification of this choice is provided below.
According to the original formulation of PD [ 13], which is now 
referred to as “Bond- based PD” (BPD), material points interact by 
exerting forces exclusively along  bond directions. This assump -
tion limited the theory's applicability to materials with Poisson's 
ratio of 0.33 for 2D problems and 0.25 for 3D problems. To over -
come these limitations, Silling et  al. introduced a significant 
generalization of the theory, known as state-  based PD [14] that 
relaxed the latter constraint on interaction force directions be -
tween material points, allowing for a more versatile modeling 
framework, thereby enabling the representation of a wider range 
of material behaviors, including hyperelastic constitutive laws. 
State- based PD is further classified into two groups based on 
the direction of interaction forces between material point pairs: 
Ordinary State-  based PD (OSB- PD) and Non-  ordinary State- 
based PD  (NOSB- PD). According to OSB- PD, material points 
exert forces in anti-  parallel directions, though not necessarily 
along their connecting bond [15]. NOSB- PD further relaxes this 
anti- parallel interaction constraint, offering greater flexibility 
in force representations. A particularly compelling feature of 
NOSB- PD is its ability to incorporate CCM constitutive laws 
into the PD framework through a process known as PD-  CCM 
correspondence [16].
This study employs NOSB- PD theory to characterize the struc -
tural mechanics underlying thromboembolism. We develop 
numerical models to capture the two predominant and compet -
ing failure mechanisms that govern thromboembolic events: (i) 
cohesive bulk fracture within the blood clot itself, and (ii) in -
terfacial debonding between the blood clot and its anchoring 
surface. Accordingly, the novel contributions of the paper are 
listed below:
1. First NOSB-  PD model for blood clots : We introduce an 
application- specific NOSB- PD model for blood clots by 
reproducing experimental force–displacement curves re -
ported by Gultekin et  al. [ 9]. We implement Ogden-  type 
strain energy functions for hyperelastic materials in three- 
dimensional geometries, extending existing PD literature 
beyond the simple material models (such as Neo-  Hookean 
formulations) and two-  dimensional geometries that have 
dominated previous studies [17, 18].
2. First clot- artificial surface interface model : We propose 
and calibrate a simple interface debonding model within 
the PD framework by reproducing experimentally obtained 
maximum detachment forces of blood clots from medically 
relevant surfaces reported by Kannojiya et al. [ 19]. To the 
best of the authors' knowledge, this is the first numerical 
model of its kind to simulate the debonding physics of a 
biological material (blood clot) from an artificial surface.
3. Computational efficiency enhancement in problems 
with symmetry : We propose generalized symmetry 
boundary conditions that accommodate multiple, non-  
axis- aligned symmetry planes in geometrically symmetric 
problems. For example, this boundary condition achieves 
a 2n- fold reduction in computational time for problems 
containing n symmetry planes. Moreover, the suggested 
boundary conditions enable the implementation of wedge-  
type boundaries comparable to those in CFD applications, 
by reformulating pointwise rotational transformations as 
planar operations.
The calibrated models will serve as input parameters for our 
future full-  scale thromboembolism simulations, enabling com -
prehensive modeling of both cohesive fracture and debond -
ing physics when investigating thromboembolism in  vivo 
and in medical devices, such as ventricular assist devices and

In CCM, quantities are defined at every point. Consequently, the 
motion of a material body is described through partial differen -
tial equations. Due to the non-  local nature of PD, mathematical 
quantities associated with a material point are deduced from the 
collective state  of the entire particle family, resulting in an inte -
gral formulation. As such, special mathematical objects called 
“states” are introduced to capture a family's collective response. 
These objects are mappings from the set ℋP to either a scalar, 
vector, or tensor value. The most common type of state is the 
vector state. For example, the reference state X contains infor -
mation on all reference bond vectors associated with all material 
points in the PD body. For more details on the formalism of PD 
states, the reader is referred to [14].

2.2   |   Hyperelasticity in Principle Stretches
In CCM, hyperelastic materials are used to represent large non- 
linear finite deformations. A brief review of these materials is

2.3   |   Modeling Adhesive Contacts

2.4.3   |   Boundary Constraints
The integral nature of the PD equation of motion makes the 
application of boundary constraints inherently challenging. 
However, boundary constraints are essential for solving phys -
ical problems. Piecewise boundary constraint assignment is 
achieved by performing a particle proximity search from the 
boundaries of the original unstructured polyhedral mesh. 
Common boundary conditions such as zero traction, fixed ve -
locity, and symmetry plane are implemented.
Symmetry plane boundary conditions, while standard in FEM 
and CFD simulations, have not received much attention in the 
PD numerical modeling literature. To the authors' knowledge, 
the only work that addresses symmetry BCs in PD simulations 
is the work by Yaghoobi and Chorzepa [ 31], which is limited to 
axis- aligned symmetry BCs. This work introduces a boundary 
condition that applies to generic planar surfaces and provides 
ways to handle particles at the intersection of multiple sym -
metry planes. A brief description of our generalized symmetry 
plane boundary condition is provided below.

An in- house parallel C++ code was developed to simulate PD prob-

4   |   Discussion
This study is a stepping stone towards realizing a computa -
tional framework that will help model the physics involved in 
the fragmentation of blood clots under fluid dynamic forces. 
Specifically, this paper demonstrates the application of PD to 
compute mechanical quantities characteristic of blood clots sub-
jected to different types of physiological forces. The accuracy of 
this PD framework was demonstrated through three test cases 
in which displacements, Von Mises stresses, and reaction forces 
were compared against FEM results computed from the solid 
mechanics solver of ANSYS.
4.1   |   Fidelity of Results
The first case—tensile loading of a plate with a hole—demon -
strated that the PD solver can accurately predict deformation val-
ues and stress concentration values in regions where expected. 
The PD-  computed Von Mises stresses and displacements were 
found to be in nearly exact agreement with FEM results, with 
the exception of slight underestimations of Von Mises stresses 
near boundaries (see Figure  6c,d). This discrepancy is probably 
due to the well-  known skin effect in PD [ 32]. Although various 
techniques proposed in the literature could be adapted to this 
model to improve accuracy, the current negligible relative error 
(less than 3%) is deemed acceptable for the intended application 
to thromboembolism which manifests a large number of greater 
uncertainties.
Flow- induced blood clots are characterized by diverse morphol -
ogies, intricate loading patterns, and complex geometrical fea -
tures including thin structural regions. To test the capability of 
our PD solver to predict the deformation field in these scenarios, 
two benchmark simulations were conducted: a thin column sub-
jected to testing loads and the tensile load of a plate with and 
without a notch. The simulation of the twisting column demon -
strated exceptional agreement between the predicted FEM and 
PD displacement fields (see Figures  7b,c and 8 ). The latter test 
case verified the ability of the numerical framework to accu -
rately compute reaction forces in slender structures subjected

4.2   |   Remarks on the Non- Local Length Scale: The 
Horizon
The original paper by Silling introduces the parameter horizon 
as a length scale that helps replace the spatial derivative in CCM 
with integral equations, where the domain of integration is ℋP 
[13]. Further studies revealed that using /u1D6FFP ≈ 3dP provides max-
imum accuracy in computing spatial derivatives—such as de -
formation gradient approximations [ 33, 34 ]. Hence, our choice 
of setting /u1D6FFP ≈ 3dP for all our numerical simulations. There are 
two schools of thought regarding the physical interpretation 
of the horizon: (i) it is a purely numerical parameter used for 
computational convenience, and (ii) it represents a fundamental 
material property intrinsic to the material being modeled [ 35]. 
We examine both interpretations within the context of different 
problem classes.
For purely elastic problems, the horizon is a numerical param -
eter. This is supported by our numerical tests for cases 1–3 
in which the horizon size was inconsequential. Once a suffi -
ciently small horizon size is chosen, subsequent refinement 
does not alter physical quantities of interest. This observation 
aligns with the theoretical result that as /u1D6FF → 0, the PD equa -
tion of motion converges to the CCM governing equations [ 36].

4.4   |   Benefits of Using the PD Framework 
for Thromboembolism
Thromboembolism is a coupled phenomenon involving the 
breakup of blood clots under fluid flow. This paper presents a 
strategy for addressing the structural aspects of thromboembo -
lism using the PD framework. PD offers two main advantages 
over traditional mesh- based methods for modeling thromboem -
bolism: (i) its integral formulation naturally handles fracture 
and discontinuities without additional computational complex -
ity, and (ii) it is a meshless method and does not face the problem 
of element distortion, and so forth. The first advantage is well es-
tablished in the fracture mechanics literature [ 29, 33]. However, 
the second advantage is particularly important for thromboem -
bolism modeling, as discussed below.
Blood clots are dynamically evolving structures that grow and 
change shape over time, both in vivo and within medical devices. 
Numerical models of thrombus growth output a time-  varying 
volume fraction field defined over an unstructured polyhedral 
mesh [48, 49]. So, if a traditional mesh-  based structural method 
is used, it would require adding new elements onto the existing 
mesh at each time step to represent the growing clot geometry, 
followed by remeshing around developing cracks and the fluid- 
clot interface. Additionally, new interpolation weights must be 
calculated between fluid and solid meshes at every time step. 
All of which would result in humongous computational costs 
when dealing with macro-  scale problems. It is exactly in these 
aspects that PD has an edge over mesh-  based methods because: 
(i) connectivity can be pre-  computed; (ii) simplified fluid force 
interpolation. We explain briefly how this can be achieved.
According to our numerical framework, a voxelized mesh must 
be generated to create the particle cloud for PD simulation (see 
Section 2.4). For the full-  scale thromboembolism problem, this 
voxelized mesh is generated based on the fluid mesh. Both the 
voxel cell-  to- cell connectivity and interpolation weights be -
tween voxel cells and the fluid mesh can be pre-  computed at 
initialization. The “new” particle generation strategy to accom -
modate growing clots involves two steps: (i) interpolate the vol -
ume fraction field difference between current and previous time 
steps from the fluid mesh onto the voxelized mesh, and (ii) gen -
erate particles in voxel cells where the volume fraction exceeds a 
specified threshold. The connectivity for each new particle can 
be grabbed from the voxel cell where it was born, avoiding the 
need for costly connectivity calculations and remeshing at each 
time step. A detailed description of this strategy will be provided 
in future work addressing the full-  scale thromboembolism 
problem with fluid flow.
5   |   Conclusion
This paper presents a critical step toward creating the first com -
prehensive numerical model of thromboembolism. This study 
successfully employed NOSB- PD theory to characterize the co -
hesive bulk fracture and debonding mechanisms of blood clots 
from artificial surfaces, including Ti, PU, and PTFE, where the 
calibrated PD parameters exhibited excellent correlation with 
published experimental data. Prior to calibrating clot-  specific 
material properties within the NOSB- PD framework, the nu -
merical methodology underwent systematic validation through 
a series of benchmark problems: (i) tensile loading of a plate 
with a hole, (ii) torsional loading of cylindrical specimens, 
and (iii) thin plate reaction analyses. The validations achieved 
exceptional quantitative agreement with ANSYS FE solu -
tions across all critical mechanical parameters, including dis -
placement fields, von-  Mises stress distributions, and reaction 
forces. The calibrated NOSB- PD parameters presented in this 
paper will serve as inputs for coupled fluid-  thrombus interac -
tion simulations to investigate complex phenomena, including 
thromboembolism and thrombolytic therapy for deep venous 
thrombosis, allowing for the development of embolic stroke 
prevention strategies in patients with prosthetic heart valves, 
ventricular assist devices, and other blood-  contacting medical 
implants.
